You have probably seen the charting method listed as one of the four classic note-taking methods, right next to Cornell and mind mapping, and then never actually tried it. Charting method note-taking is just this: your page becomes a table, with a labelled column for every category and one row per topic.

I write about productivity systems for a living and build small AI tools on the side, so I have spent a lot of time trying to make note-taking survive a real week rather than just look good in a photo.

Charting is the least-loved of the four, which is a shame, because when the material genuinely is comparable it beats the alternatives outright. If you are still weighing your options, I have [the four main note-taking methods compared](/note-taking-methods) side by side.

This guide covers what the charting method is, when it works and when it backfires, the five steps I use to set one up, a full worked example you can copy, and the mistakes that turn a good chart into an unusable one.

## What the Charting Method Actually Is

Take a blank page, turn it sideways, and rule it into three to five columns. Each column gets a label, one category of information. Each row is one topic, and every cell holds the fact where the two meet.

[IMAGE: Anatomy of a charting-method page, a ruled grid with callouts labelling the row labels down the left edge, the category headers across the top, and what belongs inside a single cell. Type: remotion. Suggested filename: charting-anatomy.png]

You will also see the same thing called grid notes or the matrix method. Both names describe one page: a grid where the columns are the questions you are asking, and the rows are the things you are asking about.

Here is the smallest useful version, taken from a project meeting:

| Decision | Owner | Deadline |
|---|---|---|
| Move the launch to March | Priya | Friday |
| Park the pricing page redesign | me | end of month |

Two rows, three columns, and you can already see who owes what. The University of York's study guide describes the method as working particularly well for subjects with clear, structured information, and as ideal for comparing facts, statistics, or different concepts.

So where does the real effort go? Not into the format. The discipline is entirely in choosing the columns.

## When the Charting Method Wins

Charting has one requirement, and it is strict. The material has to be comparable: the same kinds of facts, repeated across several things, in a shape you can predict before the session starts.

That covers more of your week than you would expect:

- Historical periods or events, compared across dates, causes, key figures and outcomes
- Competing theories or frameworks, measured against the same handful of attributes
- Drugs, conditions or procedures in a health-science class, where every entry has an onset, a cause and a treatment
- Product or tool comparisons, where you are weighing four options against four criteria
- Meetings, where each item resolves into a decision, an owner and a date

One college study-skills guide puts the trigger neatly: reach for charting when your test will focus on facts and relationships, when the course content is heavy and delivered quickly, and when you want an overview of your entire course in one spot. That last one is the sleeper benefit. A whole term of lectures can collapse into two or three sheets you can spread out on a table.

The University of Tennessee at Chattanooga's note-taking guide makes the structural point: if the lecture format is distinct (such as chronological), you may [set up your paper by drawing columns and labeling appropriate headings in a table](https://www.utc.edu/enrollment-management-and-student-affairs/center-for-academic-support-and-advisement/tips-for-academic-success/note-taking).

Meetings deserve their own mention, because that is where charting quietly earns its keep outside a classroom. A recurring meeting has a predictable agenda, which is exactly the condition the method needs. Columns for decision, owner, deadline and blocker will beat a page of prose every single time.

## When Charting Flops

Charting fails in one recognisable way: a beautiful empty grid, and a speaker who refuses to cooperate.

One note-taking guide lists the avoid cases better than most. Skip charting when:

- The speaker jumps randomly between ideas
- You are brainstorming or working an idea out from scratch
- The material needs diagrams, equations or heavy visuals
- You do not know the structure ahead of time

The University of York adds the subject-level version of the same warning: a text-based grid is [harder to work with for STEM subjects that require visual representations like graphs or formulas](https://subjectguides.york.ac.uk/note-taking/charting). A narrow cell is a terrible container for a derivation.

Narrative and argumentative material is the other bad fit, and it is the one people misjudge most often. An argument does not have columns. It has a claim, evidence, an objection and a reply, stacked in an order that matters, which is a hierarchy and belongs in [the outline method](/outlining-note-taking-method) instead.

Now the part I want to be straight with you about. Several popular guides state that the charting method improves memory and retention.

I went looking for the research behind that line while writing this, and there isn't any: every guide making the claim cites nothing at all, and I could not find a single study on charting specifically. So I am not going to repeat it.

What charting reliably gives you is structural. Comparison becomes visible, and revision gets faster, because the answer to "how did these three actually differ?" is one row you read across instead of six pages you have to reread.

## How to Use the Charting Method in 5 Steps

Five steps, in the order I actually do them. The first one carries most of the weight.

[IMAGE: The five charting steps as a compact left-to-right flow, decide columns, rule and label, short phrases, escape hatch, cover a column. Type: remotion. Suggested filename: charting-five-steps.png]

### Step 1: Decide Your Columns Before the Session, Not During It

This is the step beginners skip, and skipping it is the reason their charts fall apart. As one beginner's guide to the method puts it, charting is not a method that lets you take notes as you go: analysing your materials beforehand is an important part of the prep work.

Do it the night before, or the moment the reading lands in your inbox. Your categories are usually sitting in plain sight already, in the lecture title, the slide deck, or the chapter subheadings.

### Step 2: Rule the Columns and Label Them

Three to five columns, and no more. Past five, your cells get too narrow to write anything in.

Turn the page landscape. Label the leftmost column with whatever the row is (the topic, the case, the theory), then give every remaining column exactly one attribute. A ruler helps, and a dot-grid or squared page helps more.

### Step 3: Fill Rows With Short Phrases, Not Sentences

Two to six words per cell. Abbreviations are fine, arrows are fine, symbols are fine.

"Don't worry about writing detailed sentences. Short phrases work better," as one note-taking guide sums it up, and that single instruction is what keeps a chart readable a month later.

### Step 4: Leave an Escape Hatch

This one is my own addition, and it has rescued more charts than any other habit: leave a spare row along the bottom, or a two-finger margin down the right-hand side, for anything that fits none of your columns.

Something always fits none of them. Without a place to put it, one stray tangent breaks the whole grid.

### Step 5: Review by Covering a Column

Cover one column with your hand and try to recall its cells from the row labels alone. Then uncover it and check.

That turns the chart from a document you reread into a test you sit. [EXTERNAL_LINK_NEEDED: testing yourself beats re-reading | suggested source type: .edu learning centre or NLM/PMC article on retrieval practice] One r/NoteTaking poster described the underlying trap for note-taking in general: "The problem isn't effort. It's that passive note-taking creates the illusion of learning. You feel productive but nothing sticks."

## A Worked Example, Start to Finish

Say you are a first-year psychology or social-science student meeting research methods for the first time. Three keep coming up, and the exam is going to ask you to compare them, so the categories choose themselves: what each one is, how many people it involves, what it is good at, and where it falls down.

| Method | What it is | Sample size | What it is good at | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Survey | Asking a sample the same set of questions, by questionnaire or interview | Large, breadth is the point | Describing attitudes and behaviour across a big group | Self-reported answers, and it shows things going together, not one causing the other |
| Experiment | Changing one variable on purpose and measuring the effect on another, with participants assigned to conditions at random | Smaller, split into groups | Testing cause and effect | Controlled conditions can look nothing like real life |
| Case study | An in-depth look at one person, group or event | One case, or a small handful | Rich detail, rare situations, questions worth testing later | Findings do not generalise beyond the case |

[VERIFY: the sample-size and main-limitation characterisations in this table | source: writer's general research-methods knowledge, no external source in facts.md]

So what does the finished grid show you? Read down the sample-size column and something surfaces that no paragraph would have handed you: sample size is not a quality score, it is a consequence of what each method is for. Read across the experiment row and the trade-off sits right there in the open, since you buy causal certainty with realism.

That is the whole value of the format. Three tidy paragraphs would have carried exactly the same information and hidden both of those observations inside it.

## Paper vs. Digital: Which One to Chart On

Paper wins more often than I expected it to. Ruling the columns by hand forces you to commit to your categories before the session starts, which is precisely the discipline the method depends on, and a fixed chart on one page is genuinely pleasant to revise from.

[IMAGE: A paper notebook ruled into labelled columns lying beside a tablet showing the same chart on screen. Type: ai-prompt. Suggested filename: paper-vs-digital-chart.png]

Digital wins when you cannot predict how many rows you will need, when you want to reorder or search the thing later, or when the chart has to live somewhere your team can see it. A table in whatever notes app you already own is fine. A spreadsheet is fine. There is no special software involved here, and you do not need a new app to try this.

Someone will tell you handwriting beats screens for recall. [EXTERNAL_LINK_NEEDED: paper vs digital note-taking effectiveness | suggested source type: NLM/PMC study] For charting specifically, the difference I notice is more mundane: on paper you commit to your columns, and on a screen you keep fiddling with them.

The tablet middle ground is real too. A nursing student's charting-method notes, hand-written into a pre-made table on a tablet, did the rounds a few years ago when a note-taking app account shared them, which tells you the format travels off paper perfectly well.

If you are on paper, the page matters more than the pen. Grid or dot-grid pages turn ruling columns into a five-second job, which is one reason I fuss so much about [the best notebooks for note-taking](/best-notebooks-for-note-taking).

## Five Mistakes That Ruin a Chart

Every bad chart I have made failed in one of five ways, and four of them happened before the session even started.

[IMAGE: An overstuffed, unusable chart with far too many narrow columns and full sentences crammed into every cell, spilling over the ruled lines. Type: ai-prompt. Suggested filename: overstuffed-chart.png]

1. **Choosing your columns mid-lecture.** You end up redrawing the grid at minute twelve while the lecturer moves on without you. If you could not name the categories beforehand, this was never a charting session.
2. **Writing full sentences in the cells.** The chart stops being scannable and becomes a badly formatted essay. Short phrases, every time.
3. **Too many columns.** Eight columns on an A4 page gives you eight strips too narrow to write in. Three to five, and the rest goes in the margin.
4. **Charting material that does not chart.** One beginner's guide is blunt about the limits: charting is not meant for taking notes during live lectures (Cornell or a split-page layout suits those better), and it is not meant for equation-based information such as maths. A rambling seminar is not a grid.
5. **Building the chart and never opening it again.** This is the expensive one, and it is a note-taking problem long before it is a charting problem. As one person on r/NoteTaking described their own systems: "I have spent way too much time trying to make complex systems like Notion work, only to realize the maintenance of the app was taking more effort than the actual note taking." A chart you never cover a column of is just tidy stationery.

## How Charting Fits Next to Cornell, Outlining and Mind Mapping

Here is the one-paragraph version, since the full four-way comparison belongs in the pillar guide I linked at the top. Reach for [the Cornell note-taking method](/cornell-note-taking-method) when you cannot predict the session and need cues and a summary you can quiz yourself on afterwards. Reach for outlining when the material has a hierarchy or an argument. Reach for the [mind mapping note-taking method](/mind-mapping-note-taking-method) when you are connecting ideas or brainstorming, and the shape is a web rather than a list. Reach for charting when several things share the same set of attributes and you need to compare them.

Charting is easily the least-discussed of the four in the places students actually argue about note-taking, and I think that is a gap rather than a verdict. It is a narrow tool that is very good inside its lane.

## FAQ

### What is the charting method of note-taking?

The charting method means splitting your page into labelled columns, one per category, and giving each topic its own row, so every fact lands in the cell where its topic and its category meet. You will also see it called grid notes or the matrix method. All three names describe the same page: a table you build first and fill in second.

### When should you not use the charting method?

Skip charting whenever you cannot predict the structure of what is coming: speakers who jump between ideas, brainstorming sessions where the shape emerges as you go, material built on equations or diagrams, and narrative or argumentative content where the order of the ideas is the point. In those cases you spend the session redrawing your grid instead of listening.

### Is the charting method better than the Cornell method?

Neither is better in general, because they solve different problems. Charting compares several things across a fixed set of attributes you know in advance; Cornell captures a live session and reviews it afterwards using cues and a summary. If you cannot predict how the session will unfold, Cornell is the safer default.

### Can you use the charting method for meetings?

Yes, and it is one of the strongest non-academic uses of the method. Columns for decision, owner, deadline and blocker turn a recurring meeting into something you can scan a week later, which prose notes rarely manage. The condition is the same as in a lecture: the meeting needs a predictable agenda, so a weekly stand-up works and a freewheeling strategy session does not.

### Does the charting method improve memory?

I could not find a single study supporting a memory benefit specific to charting, and the popular guides claiming one cite nothing at all, so treat that claim as unproven. The gains you can count on are structural: comparisons become visible at a glance, and revision gets faster because the answer is a row rather than a page. The review step, covering a column and recalling its contents, is what does the actual work.
