## Meta description (draft)

`The charting method turns messy notes into a table you can actually revise from. Here's how I set one up in 5 steps, and when to skip it.`  (135 chars)

## Angle

Charting is the forgotten one of the four classic note-taking methods, and the research proves it: across 75 threads in the subreddits where students argue about note-taking, nobody mentions it once, while every article that does explain it either leaves the example table empty or ends in a pitch for a note-taking app. So this post does the two things none of the top results do together, it shows a complete, filled-in chart built from scratch on a topic a real reader might study, and it is blunt about when charting will actively hurt you (unstructured lectures, equations, narrative arguments) instead of selling it as a universal upgrade. It is also honest where the whole SERP is not: three separate sources claim charting "improves retention" with zero evidence behind any of them, and this post will say plainly that the benefit is structural, comparison becomes visible and revision gets faster, not a proven memory effect.

## Key sections (preview, becomes the outline in Phase 2)

1. What the charting method actually is (definition + the one-line "grid notes / matrix method" alias, plus a small filled example immediately so the reader sees it before reading about it)
2. When the charting method wins (comparable, repetitive, predictable material: history periods, competing theories, tool comparisons, drug/condition attributes, meeting decisions)
3. When it flops (unpredictable lectures, equations and diagrams, narrative or argumentative material, anything you cannot categorise in advance) — includes the honest note that the popular "better retention" claim is unsupported
4. How to use the charting method in 5 steps (decide categories before → draw the columns → fill rows with short phrases → leave a spillover row/margin → review by covering a column and recalling)
5. A worked example, start to finish (build one chart in full, in a domain distinct from the SERP's parasomnias/revolutions examples)
6. Paper vs. digital (ruler-and-notebook vs. a table in a docs/notes app; genuinely high-level, no app roundup) — the gap nothing in the SERP covers
7. Five mistakes that ruin a chart (picking columns mid-lecture, full sentences in cells, too many columns, charting material that does not chart, never reviewing it)
8. How charting fits next to Cornell, outlining and mind mapping (one short honest paragraph + up-link to the pillar, NOT a comparison table)
9. Conclusion + CTA (free AI tools, Text Summarizer as the pre-charting step)
10. FAQ

## Facts / data to feature prominently

- The when-to-avoid list is the spine of §3 and the most defensible content in the post: unpredictable speakers, brainstorming, equations and heavy visuals, and not knowing the structure ahead of time (facts.md §Quotes, Plaud + e-student).
- Charting requires prep: you analyse the material and choose categories BEFORE the session, not during it (facts.md §Quotes, e-student). This is the single most load-bearing practical fact in the post and the mistake most beginners make.
- Cells take short phrases, not sentences (facts.md §Quotes, Plaud).
- Charting suits comparing facts, statistics or different concepts, and is weaker where graphs and formulas are needed (facts.md §Quotes, University of York).
- **Negative fact, equally prominent:** no external source anywhere in this SERP backs any charting retention claim, and three separate sites assert one anyway (facts.md §Rejected). The post states the honest version: charting makes comparison visible and revision faster; that is the benefit, and the memory claim is unproven.
