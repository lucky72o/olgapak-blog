## Body sections

### H2 1: `What the charting method actually is`

- Covers: the plain definition (you divide the page into labelled columns, one per category, and each row is one topic), the alias line, and a small filled mini-example so the reader SEES a chart before reading about one.
- Key fact to cite: "This method works particularly well for subjects with clear, structured information or when comparing and contrasting data... It's ideal for comparing facts, statistics, or different concepts.", source: https://subjectguides.york.ac.uk/note-taking/charting (external link row 2)
- Also state: it is sometimes called **grid notes** or the **matrix method** — one line, per facts.md §Named examples; translate both on first use per audience.md §Niche jargon translation.
- Include a 3-column × 2-row mini table so the concept is concrete immediately. This is the gap in the SERP: York's example table is empty and Sheridan never shows one at all.
- Approximate word count: 200

### H2 2: `When the charting method wins`

- Covers: the material profile charting is built for — comparable, repetitive, predictable, and known in advance. Give 5 concrete scenarios rather than an abstract rule: historical periods or events, competing theories or frameworks, drug/condition attributes in a health-science class, product or tool comparisons, and meeting decisions (who owns what, by when).
- Key fact to cite: "Use the Charting Method when: Your test will focus on facts and relationships. Your course content is heavier and presented to you quickly... You want an overview of your entire course in one spot.", source: https://sheridancollege.libguides.com/takingnotesmodule/taking-notes-in-class/charting-method — **quote-and-attribute in prose only ("one college study-skills guide puts it this way"), NEVER as a link; sheridancollege.libguides.com is a top-10 SERP competitor and is not allowlist-class.**
- Cite the `.edu` source for the structural point instead: "If the lecture format is distinct (such as chronological), you may set up your paper by drawing columns and labeling appropriate headings in a table.", source: https://www.utc.edu/... (external link row 1)
- Include the meeting-notes case explicitly (secondary audience per plan.md) but keep it to 2–3 sentences; it is a thin use case in the research, not a co-equal framing.
- Approximate word count: 200

### H2 3: `When charting flops`

- Covers: the four situations where charting actively costs you — the speaker jumps around unpredictably, the material is equation- or diagram-based, the content is narrative or argumentative (an argument does not have columns), and you do not know the structure ahead of time.
- Key fact to cite: "Avoid it when: The speaker jumps randomly between ideas. You're brainstorming or ideating. The material requires diagrams, equations, or heavy visuals. You don't know the structure ahead of time.", source: facts.md §Quotes (Plaud) — **attribute in prose as "one note-taking guide", NEVER link; plaud.ai is a top-10 SERP competitor.**
- Key fact to cite: "Not suitable for all topics: Its text-based approach can be harder to work with for STEM subjects that require visual representations like graphs or formulas.", source: https://subjectguides.york.ac.uk/note-taking/charting (external link row 2, if not already spent in H2 1 — use it in whichever section reads better, once only)
- **Honesty beat (load-bearing, from plan.md §Angle):** name plainly that the "charting improves memory and retention" line repeated across several popular guides has no study behind it in any source found for this post, and that the real, defensible benefit is structural — comparison becomes visible and revision gets faster. Do NOT soften this into "studies suggest". See facts.md §Rejected / not verifiable.
- Internal link: `the outline method` → `/outlining-note-taking-method`, placed where the post says an argument or a hierarchy is better served by outlining.
- Approximate word count: 230

### H2 4: `How to use the charting method in 5 steps`

Numbered H3s. This is the section the title promises; it gets the most space.

#### H3 4.1: `Step 1: Decide your columns before the session, not during it`
- The single most load-bearing practical point in the post: charting is prep work, and every beginner gets this wrong.
- Key fact: "The charting method is not one that allows you to take notes as you go – analyzing your materials beforehand is an important part of the prep work.", source: facts.md §Quotes (e-student) — **prose attribution only, never link; e-student.org is a top-10 SERP competitor.**
- Give the timing cue no SERP result gives (research/serp.md §Angle opportunities): do it the night before, or the moment the reading/syllabus lands, using the lecture title, slide deck or chapter headings as the category source.

#### H3 4.2: `Step 2: Rule the columns and label them`
- Practical: 3–5 columns, no more; label the leftmost column with whatever the ROW is (the topic/case/theory), remaining columns are attributes.
- Landscape orientation on paper; ruler or a pre-ruled grid page.

#### H3 4.3: `Step 3: Fill rows with short phrases, not sentences`
- Key fact: "Don't worry about writing detailed sentences. Short phrases work better.", source: facts.md §Quotes (Plaud) — prose attribution only.
- Concrete instruction: 2–6 words per cell, abbreviations are fine, symbols are fine.

#### H3 4.4: `Step 4: Leave an escape hatch`
- The step nobody else includes: a spillover row or a right-hand margin for the thing that does not fit any column, so one off-topic tangent does not break the whole chart. Presented as Olga's own working practice, not as a sourced claim.

#### H3 4.5: `Step 5: Review by covering a column`
- Cover one column and try to recall its cells from the row labels; this turns the chart into a self-test rather than a document you re-read.
- Supported in spirit by the Reddit voice-of-customer theme that passive capture feels productive but does not stick: "The problem isn't effort. It's that passive note-taking creates the illusion of learning. You feel productive but nothing sticks.", source: https://www.reddit.com/r/NoteTaking/comments/1rq561w/i_spent_3_years_taking_notes_wrong_heres_what/ — **attribution rule from facts.md: this commenter was talking about note-taking generally and never mentioned charting. Quote them for the problem, never as an endorsement of this method.**
- If the writer wants to claim self-testing beats re-reading as an evidenced fact, mark `[EXTERNAL_LINK_NEEDED: testing yourself beats re-reading | suggested source type: .edu learning centre or NLM/PMC article on retrieval practice]` (external link row 4).
- Approximate word count for H2 4 including all five H3s: 350

### H2 6: `Paper vs. digital: which one to chart on`

- Covers: the open territory nothing in the studied SERP addresses (research/serp.md §Angle opportunities). High-level only, per the brief: paper wins for a fixed, small chart you will revise from and for the fact that ruling columns forces you to commit to categories; digital wins when you do not know how many rows you will need, when you want to reorder or search later, and on a tablet where you can hand-write into a pre-made table.
- Anchor it in one real observation rather than tool specs: a nursing student's charting-method notes shared publicly on a tablet, source: https://x.com/GoodnotesApp/status/1326817270195220480 (facts.md §Named examples). Reference as evidence the method survives on a tablet; do not turn it into a Goodnotes endorsement.
- If the writer wants to state anything about paper-vs-digital effectiveness as a fact, mark `[EXTERNAL_LINK_NEEDED: paper vs digital note-taking effectiveness | suggested source type: NLM/PMC study]` (external link row 3). **Do not assert a paper-beats-screens claim unsourced.**
- Internal link: `best notebooks for note-taking` → `/best-notebooks-for-note-taking`, where the post mentions grid or dot-grid pages making column ruling painless.
- **Hard constraint:** name at most a couple of generic surfaces (a notebook, a spreadsheet, a table in whatever notes app you already use). No app roundup, no comparison of note-taking apps. The brief forbids it and three of the seven SERP competitors fail exactly this way.

### H2 7: `Five mistakes that ruin a chart`

- Covers, as five short named mistakes: (1) choosing columns mid-lecture, (2) writing full sentences in cells, (3) too many columns so nothing fits, (4) charting material that does not chart (the rambling seminar), (5) building the chart and never reviewing it.
- Key fact anchor for mistake 4: "Not meant for: ...Taking notes during live lectures (use the Cornell method or split-page method for that); Information that is equation-based (math, for example)", source: facts.md §Quotes (e-student) — prose attribution only, never link.
- Voice-of-customer support for mistake 5, on systems that collapse under their own maintenance: "I have spent way too much time trying to make complex systems like Notion work, only to realize the maintenance of the app was taking more effort than the actual note taking.", source: https://www.reddit.com/r/NoteTaking/comments/1q9bqn0/looking_for_a_loweffort_reliable_notetaking/ — same attribution rule: general note-taking pain, not a charting comment.

## FAQ block

- Q: `What is the charting method of note-taking?` → A direction: one-sentence definition (page split into labelled columns by category, one row per topic), plus the grid/matrix alias.
- Q: `When should you not use the charting method?` → A direction: unpredictable speakers, equation- or diagram-heavy material, narrative arguments, and any session whose structure you cannot know in advance.
- Q: `Is the charting method better than the Cornell method?` → A direction: neither is better in general; they solve different problems (comparison vs. live capture and recall), and Cornell is the safer default when you cannot predict the session.
- Q: `Can you use the charting method for meetings?` → A direction: yes, and it is one of its best non-academic uses — columns like decision, owner, deadline, blocker — as long as the meeting has a predictable agenda.
- Q: `Does the charting method improve memory?` → A direction: answer honestly — no study was found supporting a memory benefit specifically for charting; the reliable gains are structural (comparison is visible, revision is faster), and the review step is what does the actual work.
