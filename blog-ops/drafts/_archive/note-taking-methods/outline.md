### H2 2: `The Cornell Method`

- Covers: how it works (cue column / notes area / summary strip), what it's best for (lectures, review, self-testing), honest trade-offs (weak for very fast, terminology/stat-dense sessions — counter the "no disadvantages" framing).
- Key fact: Cornell "provides a systematic format for condensing and organizing notes without laborious recopying" (paraphrase, do NOT link UTC), source: facts.md quotes. Origin: Walter Pauk, Cornell — source: facts.md named examples (may cite Cornell LSC primary, https://lsc.cornell.edu/notes.html).
- Internal link: `the full Cornell note-taking guide` → cornell-note-taking-method (published).
- Built-in-review point ties to the "capture then refine" spine.
- Approximate word count: ~330

### H2 3: `The Outline Method`

- Covers: hierarchical indented bullets (main → sub-points); best for structured/sequential content (organized lectures, textbook chapters); weak for fast or non-linear material.
- Key fact: outline suits structured content; contrast with when structure isn't clear live, source: facts.md "Method reference grid".
- Internal link: `how the outline method works in detail` → outlining-note-taking-method (live; verify at staging).
- Approximate word count: ~300

### H2 4: `The Mapping (Mind Mapping) Method`

- Covers: central topic → branching connections; visual/non-linear; best for showing relationships, brainstorming, big-picture; weak for dense factual detail or fast linear delivery. Translate "mind map" on first use.
- Key fact: "The process of rearranging linear notes into non-linear helps commit the ideas... into the long-term memory" (paraphrase, do NOT link Newcastle), source: facts.md quotes.
- Internal link: [INTERNAL_LINK_NEEDED: mind-mapping note-taking deep-dive] (post planned, not yet published).
- Approximate word count: ~300

### H2 5: `The Charting Method`

- Covers: pre-drawn columns/table, fill rows across categories; best for comparing facts across categories and relationship+fact-heavy content delivered fast; weak for unstructured/discussion content.
- Key fact: Charting fit — "Test will focus on both facts and relationships. Content is heavy and presented fast" (paraphrase, do NOT link UTC), source: facts.md named examples.
- Internal link: [INTERNAL_LINK_NEEDED: charting method deep-dive] (post planned, not yet published).
- Approximate word count: ~300

### H2 6: `Which method fits which situation`

- Covers: the situation-first decision guide (the differentiator). Start from what the reader is doing, not from a "learning style":
  - Fast, dense lecture → Cornell (cues) or minimal-signal capture, refine after.
  - Structured/organized lecture or textbook → Outline.
  - Ideas with lots of connections / brainstorming / big picture → Mapping.
  - Comparing facts across categories (fast, fact+relationship heavy) → Charting.
  - Meetings with action items → Cornell-style cue column or a light chart; the "after" matters most (exec VOC).
  - STEM vs conceptual note (answers the recurring "how does this work for math/physics/CS?" question): concepts → Cornell/Outline; fact/relationship-dense → Charting/Mapping.
- The "capture then refine" principle: don't transcribe live; capture, then condense afterward. source: facts.md quotes (reddit VOC) + Open University two-stage.
- Handwriting vs typing, done honestly: handwriting better for conceptual retention, typing better for speed/searchability/factual — retire "learning styles" (debunked). 
- Key facts: Frontiers/NTNU handwriting study (link, confirm wording Stage 3d) + Mueller & Oppenheimer longhand>laptop ([EXTERNAL_LINK_NEEDED:] DOI) + UNC 24-hour review claim (state, don't link). source: facts.md statistics.
- Approximate word count: ~470 (largest section)

### H2 7: `The step most people skip: what to do with your notes after`

- Covers: the honest bridge — any method only works if you go back and condense/quiz/resurface; that "after" work is where notes actually stick (VOC: summarizing is the real work, people reach for summary tools). Soft, single Text Summarizer mention: when you've got pages of notes or a long reading to distill, a free summarizer can speed the condense step (match tool to topic; no feature dump).

## FAQ block

- Q: `What is the best note-taking method?` → A direction: there's no single best — it depends on the content and task; give the one-line chooser (Cornell for lectures/review, Outline for structured content, Mapping for connections, Charting for comparisons).
- Q: `Which note-taking method is best for studying / for exams?` → A direction: Cornell for its built-in cue-and-summary review; emphasize the capture-then-refine + self-quiz habit over the method itself.
- Q: `Is handwriting or typing notes better?` → A direction: handwriting tends to help conceptual retention; typing wins on speed and searchability — pick by task, and note "learning styles" is not the right basis.
- Q: `What is the Cornell note-taking method?` → A direction: one-sentence definition (cue column, notes area, summary) + point to the Cornell deep-dive.
- Q: `Can I combine note-taking methods?` → A direction: yes — most people mix (e.g., Outline live, Cornell cues + summary after); methods are tools, not rules.
